WCAG 2.2 and Medical Device Interface Safety

WCAG addresses digital accessibility. Medical device usability engineering addresses use-related risk. Safe, accessible interfaces require both disciplines and separate evidence for each.

Executive summary

WCAG 2.2 is the latest completed Web Content Accessibility Guidelines standard. Its testable criteria address whether digital content is perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. It provides an appropriate accessibility baseline for web-based medical products, patient portals, clinician dashboards, electronic instructions, and connected-device applications.

WCAG is not a medical device safety standard. It neither identifies all hazardous situations nor establishes acceptable residual risk, critical tasks, or safe and effective use under realistic conditions. Those functions belong primarily to usability engineering under IEC 62366-1, risk management under ISO 14971, applicable regulations, and human factors validation.

Scope distinction

WCAG evaluates whether disabled people can perceive, operate, and understand content and whether assistive technologies can interpret it. Medical device usability engineering evaluates whether intended users can complete safety-critical tasks without unacceptable use-related risk. The scopes overlap but remain distinct.

A low-contrast dose value may violate WCAG and contribute to an overdose. WCAG identifies the barrier; the medical device process connects it to the user, task, use error, hazardous situation, potential harm, risk control, and validation evidence. A missing transcript may be a serious accessibility failure without a credible safety consequence. Accessibility obligations extend beyond safety, while safety analysis must include barriers that can contribute to harm.

Principal findings

  1. Use WCAG 2.2 as the current completed web accessibility baseline. Do not base conformance claims on the WCAG 3 draft.
  2. Assess accessibility barriers as potential contributors to use error when harm is plausible for the relevant user, task, and context.
  3. Maintain separate accessibility and usability engineering claims connected through shared requirements and traceability.
  4. Validate safety-critical interactions with representative users under realistic conditions. Automated tests and criterion-level verification are insufficient.

Scope and method

This report provides an educational synthesis for teams designing web-based or software-mediated medical device interfaces. Its scope includes patient portals, clinician dashboards, connected-device applications, electronic instructions for use, and similar interfaces in which accessibility barriers may affect task completion or safety.

The analysis used four steps:

  1. Establish the accessibility baseline. Review WCAG 2.2, its conformance model, relevant W3C guidance, and its ISO publication.
  2. Map related obligations. Compare WCAG with medical device usability engineering, risk management, human-centred design, regulatory guidance, and software accessibility standards.
  3. Trace barriers to risk. Determine whether an accessibility barrier could contribute to a foreseeable use error, hazardous situation, or harm in a defined context.
  4. Define an integrated practice. Establish a workflow and evidence model that connects accessibility and safety without treating them as equivalent.

Sources consist primarily of standards, regulatory documents, and official guidance available through 25 September 2026. This report is not a systematic review, device assessment, or conformity determination.

Current WCAG standard

As of 25 September 2026, WCAG 2.2, dated 12 December 2024, is the latest completed W3C Recommendation. WCAG 3 remains a Working Draft and is not a basis for conformance claims. W3C recommends applying WCAG 2.2.

Its thirteen guidelines are organized under four principles:

  • Perceivable: information is available through suitable sensory channels and formats, including text alternatives, captions, semantic structure, adaptable layouts, and sufficient contrast.
  • Operable: navigation and actions support relevant input methods, focus, timing, motion, gestures, and target sizes.
  • Understandable: content and behavior are readable, predictable, and supported by clear input and authentication guidance.
  • Robust: browsers and assistive technologies receive reliable names, roles, values, and status information.

WCAG defines three conformance levels. Level A is the minimum baseline; Level AA includes all A and AA criteria and is the level most policies apply; Level AAA includes all three levels. W3C does not recommend requiring whole-site AAA conformance because some content cannot satisfy every AAA criterion.

Conformance applies to complete pages and every view within a complete process, such as registration, device pairing, configuration, review, and confirmation. Automated tools identify some patterns, but evaluation requires human judgment. Assistive technology testing and participation by disabled users identify issues that automated rules cannot.

Edition details matter

W3C reports that ISO/IEC 40500:2025 is identical to the October 2023 WCAG 2.2 publication. Contracts and regulatory records should identify the edition, date, conformance level, scope, and relied-upon technologies rather than state only “WCAG compliant.”

Changes in WCAG 2.2

WCAG 2.2 added nine success criteria addressing cognitive and learning disabilities, low vision, and limited fine-motor control. It also removed the obsolete 4.1.1 Parsing criterion.

AA

2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured

Author-created content must not fully obscure a keyboard-focused component.

AAA

2.4.12 Focus Not Obscured

The enhanced criterion requires the complete focused component to remain visible.

AAA

2.4.13 Focus Appearance

Focus indicators must meet defined minimum area and contrast-change requirements.

AA

2.5.7 Dragging Movements

Dragging requires a single-pointer alternative unless it is essential or provided by the user agent.

AA

2.5.8 Target Size

Pointer targets generally require a minimum size of 24 × 24 CSS pixels or sufficient spacing, subject to defined exceptions.

A

3.2.6 Consistent Help

Repeated help mechanisms must retain the same relative order across a set of pages.

A

3.3.7 Redundant Entry

Previously supplied information must be populated or selectable, subject to essential, security, and validity exceptions.

AA

3.3.8 Accessible Authentication

Authentication must not rely solely on a cognitive-function test unless an allowed alternative or assistance mechanism is available.

AAA

3.3.9 Accessible Authentication

The enhanced criterion removes the object-recognition and personal-content exceptions available at Level AA.

Two distinctions are important. Level AA does not universally require 44 × 44 pixel controls: its target-size criterion specifies 24 × 24 CSS pixels or sufficient spacing, with exceptions. The 44 × 44 requirement applies at Level AAA. Accessible authentication also does not prohibit passwords; it permits password managers, paste, or an alternative method that avoids a cognitive test.

    Accessibility as a safety factor

    Disability is part of the intended-use context. Users may have permanent visual, auditory, motor, speech, or cognitive disabilities. Injury, medication, stress, low light, noise, personal protective equipment, and illness may also create temporary or situational limitations. Aging and comorbidities may combine several constraints.

    IEC 62366-1 addresses safety in normal use, including correct use and use error. ISO 14971 provides the broader life-cycle risk management process. Identifying an accessibility failure is therefore insufficient: the team must determine whether it can contribute to a hazardous situation or harm, define controls, and verify their effectiveness.

    Comparison of accessibility and medical device safety scopes.
    QuestionAccessibility lensSafety/usability lens
    Primary outcomeEquivalent access to digital content and functions.Safe and effective use with controlled use-related risk.
    Unit of analysisPages, views, components, content, and complete processes.Users, tasks, interfaces, environments, use errors, hazardous situations, and harms.
    EvidenceInspection, manual testing, automated support, assistive technology, and research with disabled users.Use specification, risk analysis, formative evaluation, verification, human factors validation, and post-market data.
    Limit of evidenceWCAG conformance does not establish acceptable medical risk.Human factors validation does not establish WCAG conformance or equivalent access.

    Risk controls should prioritize inherently clear interactions, such as unambiguous values, alternatives to dragging, and persistent confirmation summaries. These controls are generally stronger than warnings or training that depend on memory. Trade-offs remain: authentication must balance access and unauthorized use, timeouts and stale data, and audible cues and alarm burden. ISO 14971 provides the process for assessing these competing risks.

    Standards and regulatory framework

    No single document covers accessibility, human factors, software quality, electrical safety, labeling, and market-specific law. The relevant framework has four layers.

    1. Accessibility requirements

    WCAG 2.2 is normative for web content. WCAG2ICT explains how Level A and AA criteria may apply to non-web documents and software. It is an informative W3C Group Note, does not establish non-web WCAG conformance, and excludes hardware. ISO 9241-171:2025 provides broader software accessibility requirements. EN 301 549 extends accessibility requirements to ICT products and services beyond WCAG.

    2. Human-centred and safety-related usability

    ISO 9241-210:2019 structures human-centred design across the life cycle but does not address health and safety in detail. IEC 62366-1:2015+A1:2020 defines safety-focused medical device usability engineering. IEC 60601-1-6 applies that process to medical electrical equipment and basic safety or essential performance.

    3. Risk, quality, and product life cycle

    ISO 14971:2019 governs medical device risk management across the life cycle. ISO 13485 places design and development within the quality management system. IEC 62304 addresses medical device software life-cycle processes, while IEC 82304-1 addresses health software product safety. None replaces an accessibility specification.

    4. Regulatory and market context

    Annex I of the EU Medical Device Regulation requires manufacturers to reduce use-error risks related to ergonomic features and intended use environments. It also requires consideration of intended users’ knowledge, experience, training, and medical or physical conditions, including disability. FDA’s August 2026 human factors guidance similarly addresses intended users, uses, environments, critical tasks, and realistic validation. Although guidance is not a binding standard, it informs expected evidence.

    European citation status

    EN 301 549 V4.1.1, published in September 2026, adopts WCAG 2.2 for relevant web, document, and software content. As of 25 September 2026, it had not been cited in the EU Official Journal. For public-sector websites and mobile applications under Directive (EU) 2016/2102, Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/1339 still cited V3.2.1. The European Accessibility Act follows a separate harmonized-standards process; V4.1.1 did not automatically establish a presumption of conformity.

    The European Accessibility Act applies to specified consumer products and services from 28 June 2025; it is not a general medical device rule. An EU harmonized standard provides presumption of conformity only for cited requirements and editions. Each product, channel, and market therefore requires a scope and legal-status assessment.

    Integrated workflow

    Accessibility should be incorporated into the same traceable development system as safety and quality.

    1. Define the context.Document intended users, disabilities, aging, temporary limitations, assistive technologies, health literacy, languages, stress, lighting, noise, personal protective equipment, connectivity, and the physical environment.
    2. Establish connected requirements.Convert applicable WCAG, EN 301 549, and ISO 9241-171 provisions into verifiable design inputs traceable to components, views, content, and complete processes.
    3. Include safety-relevant barriers in risk analysis.Trace each plausible barrier from user and task to use error, hazardous situation, and harm. Identify hazard-related use scenarios and critical tasks under IEC 62366-1 and ISO 14971.
    4. Prioritize interface controls.Prefer designs that prevent errors or make them apparent. Use labels, warnings, and training as supporting controls rather than substitutes for correctable interface defects.
    5. Conduct formative evaluation.Study representative disabled users with relevant assistive technologies. Include interruptions, degraded conditions, setup, recovery, maintenance, and uncommon critical paths.
    6. Verify accessibility.Combine automated tools, manual keyboard testing, zoom and reflow review, assistive technology testing, content review, and complete-process inspection. Record scope, version, methods, findings, exceptions, and remediation.
    7. Validate safety-critical use separately.Use representative intended users, the final interface, realistic conditions, and all critical tasks. Accessibility verification informs but does not replace validation.
    8. Monitor after release.Incorporate accessibility defects, support data, use errors, complaints, and field signals into production and post-production monitoring, corrective and preventive action, change control, and the risk file.

    Evidence and traceability

    Research, design inputs, and defect data may be shared while supporting distinct claims. The accessibility record identifies evaluated content and functions, applicable requirements, technologies, and methods. The usability engineering file documents use specifications, known or foreseeable hazards, hazard-related use scenarios, interface specifications, formative evaluation, validation, and post-market findings.

    Traceability connects the records. For example, a requirement that every dose-adjustment action provide keyboard and single-pointer alternatives may map to WCAG 2.1.1 and 2.5.7, an interface design input, risk controls, verification cases, formative observations, and a validation scenario. Shared evidence may support several decisions, but the resulting claims remain separate.

    Review question

    Can the team identify who depends on a requirement, for which task and environment, the consequences of failure, and how the associated risk is controlled and monitored?

    Representative participation is essential. A screen reader check by a sighted developer supports verification but does not replace research with experienced screen reader users. A laboratory task performed by a healthy employee is not representative solely because the device is complete. Sampling should reflect intended-user characteristics that can affect interaction.

    Recommendations

    1. Adopt WCAG 2.2 AA as the named baseline when web content is in scope.Define included products, views, complete processes, technologies, exceptions, and required evidence.
    2. Include accessibility characteristics in the use specification.Record relevant sensory, physical, and cognitive characteristics, assistive technologies, environmental constraints, and foreseeable adaptations.
    3. Assess accessibility barriers in use-related risk analysis.Include a barrier when a credible chain connects it to a use error, hazardous situation, or harm. Do not classify every accessibility defect as a safety issue.
    4. Define shared, testable design inputs.Translate applicable WCAG criteria and risk controls into verifiable and traceable interface requirements.
    5. Evaluate complete processes with representative disabled users.Combine manual accessibility evaluation, formative research, and separate validation of safety-critical tasks under realistic conditions.
    6. Maintain distinct, connected evidence records.Connect accessibility records and the usability engineering file through requirements, defects, risk controls, verification, and change control.
    7. Review external requirements throughout the life cycle.Record editions and jurisdictional status, and reassess them during design changes, release planning, and post-market monitoring.

    Limitations

    • Avoid “WCAG-certified” without a defined certification scheme. A defensible conformance claim identifies the WCAG version, level, date, scope, and relied-upon technologies.
    • Do not equate conformance with usability. A conforming process may still be confusing, inefficient, clinically inappropriate, or unsafe.
    • Do not equate validation with accessibility. A human factors validation study may omit systematic barriers and does not assess every WCAG criterion or complete process.
    • Do not rely solely on automation. Tools cannot reliably assess meaning, all reading-order cases, alternative quality, error clarity, or interaction quality with assistive technology.
    • Do not use participants to confirm known defects. Correct objective failures before research and use participant time to study context, strategies, trade-offs, and residual uncertainty.
    • Maintain the standards map. Record edition dates and citation status because standards, regulatory guidance, and legal references change independently.

    WCAG provides precise terminology and testable requirements for accessibility barriers. Medical device usability engineering adds the traceable relationship among user, task, use error, hazardous situation, harm, control, and evidence. Applying both disciplines produces stronger accessibility and safety claims without conflating them.
